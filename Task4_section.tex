\section{Task 4: Exploring the Hospital Length-of-Stay Data}

\subsection{Data Overview}
The second engagement concerns hospital capacity planning. The data set
(\texttt{mimic3d.csv}, derived from the MIMIC-III critical-care database)
contains \textbf{58{,}976 hospital admissions and 28 variables}. Each record is
one admission and combines patient demographics, admission details, measures
of how intensively the hospital interacted with the patient, and our target
variable: \texttt{LOSdays}, the length of stay in days.
Table~\ref{tab:mimic_dictionary} summarizes the main variable groups.

Three features matter for the modelling in Task~5. First, the distribution is
\textbf{heavily right-skewed} (skewness $\approx 4.6$): the median stay is
about 6.5 days, but a long tail reaching 295 days pulls the mean up to
10.1 days (Figure~\ref{fig:los_hist}). Second, \textbf{89.7\% of admissions
already exceed two days}, a useful empirical baseline for the probability
question posed in Task~5. Third, 128 admissions record a stay of exactly
zero days, which must be handled before any logarithmic transformation.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{task4_los_hist.pdf}
\caption{Distribution of length of stay (axis truncated at 60 days; the tail
extends to 295 days). The solid line marks the median, the dashed line the mean.}
\label{fig:los_hist}
\end{figure}

A skewed, strictly positive outcome is a poor fit for a normal likelihood on
the raw scale. Figure~\ref{fig:los_log} shows that after a log
transformation the distribution becomes approximately normal: the skewness
falls from $4.6$ to $-0.5$ and the histogram closely tracks the fitted normal
curve. \textbf{This justifies modelling \(\log(\texttt{LOSdays})\) with a
normal likelihood and normal priors in Task~5}, transforming back to days to
answer probability questions such as \(P(\text{LOS} > 2)\).

\subsection{Length of Stay Across Admission Types}
Figure~\ref{fig:los_by_type} and Table~\ref{tab:los_by_type} break the stays
down by admission type. Emergency admissions dominate the data (71\% of
records). Urgent admissions stay longest (median 8.1 days). Newborns are a
distinctive subgroup: they show the \emph{shortest median} (4.1 days) but a
\emph{high mean} (11.5 days), because most newborns go home quickly while a
minority of intensive-care cases stay for weeks. Gender, by contrast, makes
essentially no difference (median 6.4 vs.\ 6.5 days for women and men).

\subsection{Numeric Predictors and Data Quality}
Two findings shape how the interaction variables should be used in Task~5.
First, all linear correlations with \texttt{LOSdays} are \textbf{weak}
($|r| \le 0.21$; the strongest are \texttt{NumCallouts} at $-0.21$ and
\texttt{NumRx} at $-0.17$), so no single predictor explains length of stay by
itself. Second, the \texttt{Num*} columns contain non-integer values (e.g.\
14.91 prescriptions), revealing that they are \textbf{per-day rates rather
than raw counts}: the totals have already been divided by the length of stay.
This explains the negative correlations -- short stays tend to be intense,
with many interactions per day -- and it means the signs of the corresponding
regression coefficients must be interpreted with care.

Missingness is mild and concentrated in demographic fields:
\texttt{marital\_status} (10{,}128 records), \texttt{religion} (458) and
\texttt{AdmitDiagnosis} (25). In addition, \texttt{AdmitProcedure} encodes
missing values as the literal string \texttt{"na"} in 8{,}032 records, which
a standard missing-value check would silently overlook.

\textbf{Takeaways for Task~5.} Model length of stay on the log scale with
normal priors; drop or offset the 128 zero-day stays before taking logs;
expect a high posterior probability for \(P(\text{LOS} > 2)\), since 89.7\%
of observed stays already exceed two days; and treat the per-day
\texttt{Num*} rates as covariates whose signs reflect intensity of care, not
duration.
